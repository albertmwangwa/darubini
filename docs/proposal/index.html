Darubini

A malaria microscopy copilot. A phone on the eyepiece, SAM 3 finds every parasite and white blood cell, the count follows the WHO thick-film rule, and the report arrives in Kiswahili or English for the microscopist to confirm.

The problem

Counting parasites is slow, skilled work, and the burden is heaviest here

282Mestimated malaria cases worldwide in 2024
610,000estimated malaria deaths in 2024; 11 African countries carry about two-thirds
4.3%of global malaria deaths were in Tanzania, fourth highest worldwide

Light microscopy of Giemsa-stained thick films is the reference method for confirming malaria and measuring parasite density. Microscopists count parasites against 200 to 500 white blood cells, field by field. It is tiring, and accuracy drops with fatigue, stain quality and smear thickness. Sources: WHO World Malaria Report 2025; The Citizen.

The solution

Six steps from eyepiece to confirmed count

  1. Capture

    Phone on the eyepiece photographs one field at a time.

  2. Quality gate

    Focus, exposure and stain checks reject bad fields.

  3. Segment

    SAM 3 finds parasites and WBC from concept and exemplar prompts.

  4. Count

    WHO rule: 200 WBC, extend to 500 if few parasites.

  5. Report

    Muse Spark reviews quality and writes in Kiswahili or English.

  6. Confirm

    The microscopist checks flagged fields and signs off.

Evaluation and safety

Measured honestly, used responsibly

What we measure

  • Detection precision, recall and F1 for parasites and WBC
  • Count error, bias and correlation against expert labels
  • Slide sensitivity and specificity on Tanzanian slides
  • Ablations: baseline, SAM 3 text, SAM 3 with exemplars, plus Muse Spark QC

How we keep it safe

  • Decision support only; a microscopist confirms every result
  • Numbers come from deterministic code, never from the language model
  • Unusable and doubtful fields are flagged, not hidden
  • Limits published in a model card; no patient identifiers stored

Density uses an assumed 8,000 WBC per microlitre, as in standard thick-film practice. Darubini has not been clinically validated.
